# Symptoms of a Failing Garage Door Opener Capacitor

You press the button and hear the opener hum, click, or buzz, but the door barely moves, or doesn't move at all. The light comes on, the remote works, and the opener sounds like it's trying. Many homeowners assume the whole opener has died.

Sometimes it has. But in many cases the culprit is a small, inexpensive part inside the motor unit called a capacitor. Here's what a capacitor does, the symptoms of one that's failing, how to tell it apart from other problems, and what to do about it.

---

## What a Capacitor Does in a Garage Door Opener

Many garage door openers, especially older ones and many with AC motors, use a capacitor to help the motor start and run. Starting a motor takes a jolt of extra power, and the capacitor stores electrical energy and releases it to give the motor that kick.

When the capacitor weakens, the motor doesn't get that boost. It may hum without turning, start slowly, or struggle to lift the door. As the capacitor continues to degrade, the symptoms get worse until the opener stops working altogether.

Not every opener has one. Many newer openers use DC motors with electronic controls, which work differently, so the symptoms below apply mostly to older and AC-motor units.

---

## The Most Common Symptoms

**1. The motor hums but the door doesn't move.**
This is the classic symptom. You hit the button, the opener hums or buzzes, and nothing happens. The trolley doesn't travel, or the door barely twitches.

**2. The door moves slowly or weakly.**
The opener starts, but the door crawls up or down more slowly than it used to. A capacitor that can deliver only part of its rating produces a motor that has less power than it should.

**3. The opener needs help to get started.**
Some failing capacitors leave the motor unable to start by itself. Technicians sometimes see motors that run once they've been given a push, then stall again on the next cycle.

**4. The door stops partway.**
The opener starts, moves the door a short distance, and then stops, often without reversing. The motor doesn't have the power to complete the cycle.

**5. Inconsistent operation.**
It works fine one day and not the next. It works in the afternoon but struggles in the morning. Weak capacitors tend to be unpredictable, which makes them frustrating.

**6. The motor runs hot or smells like it's burning.**
A struggling motor works harder and generates more heat. If you notice a hot smell, shut the opener off and unplug it.

**7. The motor clicks or buzzes but the lights and remote work normally.**
The logic board is receiving the signal and the light is working, but the motor isn't getting what it needs to turn. That points toward the motor circuit, and the capacitor is a common cause.

## A Bulging or Leaking Capacitor

If you've ever seen the inside of an opener, a bad capacitor is sometimes obvious. A failed capacitor may look swollen, bulging, or may have leaked fluid. Don't open the motor housing to look, though, for reasons covered below. Leave inspection to someone with the training and tools.

---

## Don't Assume It's the Capacitor: Other Causes With Similar Symptoms

A humming motor and a door that won't move can have several causes, and the capacitor is only one of them. Before anyone replaces a part, it's worth ruling out other possibilities:

**A broken spring.** This is by far the most common reason a door won't lift. If a spring is broken, the door is too heavy for the opener, and the opener may hum or strain. Check the balance by disconnecting the opener and lifting the door by hand. If it's very heavy, the springs are the problem.

**A disconnected trolley.** If the red emergency release cord was pulled, the opener may run without moving the door.

**A stripped drive gear.** Worn gears inside the opener can let the motor spin while the door doesn't move. This often makes a grinding or whirring sound.

**A failing motor.** Sometimes the motor itself is wearing out.

**An obstruction or a stuck door.** A door that's frozen to the floor, off track, or jammed will make the opener struggle.

**A logic board problem.** A failing control board can cause erratic operation that looks like a motor problem.

Because these can look so similar, a proper diagnosis matters. Replacing the wrong part wastes money and time.

---

## Why You Shouldn't Open the Motor Housing Yourself

A capacitor can hold an electrical charge even after the opener has been unplugged. Touching the terminals can deliver a painful shock. Opening the motor housing exposes live electrical components.

Some YouTube tutorials make capacitor replacement look easy, and for someone with the right training and tools it can be. But for most homeowners, it's not a safe DIY repair. If you suspect a capacitor, the safest thing to do is unplug the opener, stop using it, and have it looked at.

You can safely do these things yourself:

- Unplug the opener or switch off its breaker
- Pull the emergency release cord and operate the door manually if you need to get in or out, once you've confirmed the door itself is balanced and moves freely
- Check the balance of the door and look for obvious mechanical problems
- Note the opener's brand, model, and approximate age

---

## Repair or Replace?

If a capacitor is the confirmed cause, replacing it is usually inexpensive compared with a new opener. Still, a few things are worth weighing.

**Opener age.** If your opener is 12 to 15 years old or more, other parts may be nearing the end of their life too. A capacitor repair might buy you time, but you could be back in a year with another problem.

**Features and safety.** Newer openers include better safety features, quieter operation, battery backup, and smart controls. If yours is older and basic, replacement might be worth considering.

**Overall condition.** If the opener has other issues, such as grinding gears, a noisy motor, or a flaky control board, replacing the whole unit often makes more sense than repairing one part at a time.

**The cost of the repair relative to the opener.** If the repair cost approaches the price of a new opener, replacement is usually the better value.

An honest technician will tell you whether a repair makes sense or whether you'd be better off putting that money toward a new opener.

---

## What to Do If You Think Your Capacitor Is Failing

1. **Stop using the opener** if it's humming, smelling hot, or clearly struggling.
2. **Unplug it.**
3. **Check the door's balance** and look for obvious mechanical problems. A broken spring is the most common cause of a door that won't lift.
4. **Use the emergency release cord** to operate the door manually if you need to, and only if the door is balanced and moves freely.
5. **Call a technician** to diagnose it properly.

---

A weak capacitor can look like a dead opener, but it's often a repairable part. The important thing is to get the diagnosis right before spending money, and to stay out of the motor housing.
