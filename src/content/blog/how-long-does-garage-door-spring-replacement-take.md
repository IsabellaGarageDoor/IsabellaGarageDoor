# How Long Does Garage Door Spring Replacement Take?

If your garage door spring just broke, you're probably standing in your garage with a car you can't get out and a question running through your head: how long is this going to take?

The short answer is that a straightforward torsion spring replacement takes a trained technician between 45 minutes and 90 minutes from arrival to finished. Most jobs land closer to an hour.

But like most things in home repair, the real answer is a little more nuanced than that. Here's what actually determines how long your spring replacement will take — and what can make it go faster or slower.

---

## The Baseline: What a Standard Job Looks Like

A typical residential garage door has one or two torsion springs mounted on a horizontal shaft above the door. The most common configuration on a single car door is one spring. Double car doors usually run two springs side by side on the same shaft.

For a standard two-spring replacement on a double car door with no complications, here's roughly how the time breaks down:

- **Assessment and setup** — 5 to 10 minutes. A technician will look at the full door system before touching anything, check the spring specs, inspect the cables, drums, and bearing plates, and confirm what needs to be done.
- **Removing the old springs** — 10 to 15 minutes. The tension has to be carefully released before anything can be removed. This is the most technically demanding part of the job.
- **Installing the new springs and tensioning** — 20 to 30 minutes. New springs go on, cables are checked and reseated if needed, and the springs are wound to the correct tension for the door's weight.
- **Testing and adjustment** — 10 to 15 minutes. The door gets run through several cycles, the balance is checked, and any fine adjustments are made until the door operates correctly.

That's your 45 to 90 minute window under normal conditions.

---

## What Makes It Take Longer

Several factors can push a spring job past the 90-minute mark.

**Cable damage.** When a spring breaks, it often takes a cable with it — or the sudden loss of spring tension causes a cable to jump off the drum. If cables need to be replaced or reseated, that adds time to the job. It's not uncommon for a spring replacement to turn into a spring-and-cable job once the technician gets a look at everything.

**Corroded or seized hardware.** On older doors, the bolts and set screws that hold the spring hardware in place can rust solid over time. A fastener that should take 30 seconds to remove can take considerably longer if it's seized. This is especially common on doors that haven't been serviced or lubricated in many years.

**Non-standard spring sizes.** Most residential doors use common spring sizes that any well-stocked technician will have on hand. Occasionally a door has an unusual configuration — an extremely heavy door, a non-standard shaft setup, or a spring size that isn't commonly stocked. If the right spring isn't on the truck, parts sourcing adds time.

**Opener issues discovered during testing.** Sometimes a spring replacement reveals that the opener has been working against a failing spring for a long time and is now struggling even with a new spring installed. If opener adjustments or repairs are needed, that extends the job.

**Extension springs instead of torsion springs.** Some older or lower-clearance garage doors use extension springs — the long springs that run horizontally along the sides of the tracks rather than a single shaft above the door. Extension spring replacement is a different process and can take longer depending on the configuration.

---

## What Makes It Go Faster

A well-maintained door in good condition makes any spring job go more smoothly.

If the hardware is in good shape, the cables are intact, and the door is otherwise operating correctly, a technician can focus entirely on the spring swap without detours. A door that's been lubricated regularly and had its hardware kept tight is simply easier to work on.

Having the right spring on hand also matters. A technician who stocks a broad range of spring sizes and arrives prepared for your door's specifications can start the job immediately rather than sourcing parts mid-call.

---

## Why Spring Replacement Isn't a DIY Job

It's worth addressing this directly because it comes up often. Torsion springs are under extreme tension — hundreds of pounds of stored force in a coiled steel spring. Releasing and winding that tension requires specific tools called winding bars, and the process has a real margin for error. A spring that slips during winding, or winding bars that aren't the right size, can cause the spring to release violently.

This isn't a scare tactic — it's why professional technicians use the tools they do and why spring replacement is consistently cited as one of the more dangerous DIY home repair attempts. The time and cost savings of doing it yourself aren't worth the risk.

---

## What About Same-Day Service?

For most residential spring replacements, same-day service is realistic if you call during normal business hours. A technician who stocks common spring sizes can typically arrive, complete the job, and have your door operational the same day you call.

If your spring broke overnight or on a weekend, turnaround depends on the technician's schedule and whether they offer after-hours calls. For a door you genuinely can't operate — car trapped inside, door stuck partially open — most technicians treat it as a priority.

---

## The Full Picture

Here's the realistic summary:

- **Simple single spring replacement:** 45 to 60 minutes
- **Standard double spring replacement:** 60 to 90 minutes
- **Spring replacement with cable work:** 90 minutes to 2 hours
- **Complicated configurations or parts sourcing:** Add time accordingly

A broken spring feels like a big deal when it happens — and it stops your day cold. But it's one of the more routine repairs in the trade, and under normal conditions it's resolved in about the time it takes to watch a movie.
