# Getting Your Garage Door Ready for Back to School

If your teenager is getting home before you this school year, the garage door is probably how they're getting inside. That makes it worth a few minutes of attention before September arrives — not because anything is likely wrong, but because a door that's working perfectly is something you never think about, and a door that isn't is a problem at exactly the wrong time of day.

Here's a practical checklist for getting your garage door ready for the school year.

---

## Start Here: Why a Keypad Is Worth It

If your teenager is currently using a hidden spare key to get inside, a wireless keypad is a meaningful upgrade worth considering before school starts.

A physical key can be lost, forgotten at school, or borrowed by a friend and never returned. A keypad code can't be lost, and if it ever gets shared with someone it shouldn't be, you change it in thirty seconds from your phone or at the opener itself. No locksmith, no replacing the key, no wondering whether the old one is still floating around somewhere.

Keypads mount on the outside of the garage door frame and run on a standard 9-volt battery. Most are compatible with the major opener brands — LiftMaster, Chamberlain, Craftsman, Genie — and programming one takes about five minutes once you have it in hand. For teenagers it becomes second nature almost immediately.

If you already have a keypad, skip ahead. If you don't, it's worth adding to the back-to-school list alongside new backpacks and school supplies.

---

## Check the Keypad Battery

If you already have a keypad, check the battery before school starts.

Most wireless keypads run on AAA or 9-volt batteries and will function normally right up until the battery dies — there's usually no warning, no slow fade, just a keypad that suddenly stops working. Finding this out when your teenager is standing outside in January isn't ideal.

Pull the cover off the keypad, swap in a fresh 9-volt, and you're done. It takes two minutes and eliminates one of the more common reasons a keypad stops responding. If you can't remember the last time you changed it, change it now.

While you're at it, check the batteries in your handheld remotes too. A remote that's been sitting in a hot car all summer may be weaker than it was in April.

## Consider MyQ Smart Control

If your opener is a LiftMaster or Chamberlain made in the last several years, there's a good chance it's MyQ compatible — or already has MyQ built in. MyQ is a smart home platform that connects your garage door opener to a smartphone app, and for parents with teenagers coming home alone it's genuinely useful.

With MyQ you can open or close the garage door from anywhere using your phone. You also get notifications when the door opens or closes, so you know when your teenager got home without having to text them. If the door gets left open, you can close it remotely from work or wherever you are.

You may have already encountered MyQ without knowing it — Amazon's Key in-garage delivery feature uses the same technology to open your door for package delivery. The same capability works for letting your teenager in if they ever forget the keypad code or their phone dies and they need you to open it remotely.

MyQ devices are available as add-on accessories for compatible openers and are relatively inexpensive. If your opener is older and not compatible, it's a feature worth considering when the time comes for a replacement.

## Test the Safety Features

This one matters more when teenagers are using the garage independently. Modern garage door openers have two safety systems designed to prevent the door from closing on a person, a pet, or anything else in its path. Both should be confirmed working before your kids are coming home on their own.

**The photo eye test.** Your opener has two small sensors mounted near the floor on either side of the door opening — one sends an infrared beam, the other receives it. When something breaks that beam while the door is closing, the door should stop and reverse immediately. Test it by activating the close cycle and slowly moving your foot into the path of the beam. The door should stop and reverse before making contact with your foot.

**The auto-reverse test.** Place a piece of 2x4 lumber flat on the ground in the center of the doorway. Close the door and watch what happens when it contacts the board. The door should reverse immediately upon contact — within a second or two of touching the wood.

Both of these tests take five minutes total. If either one doesn't behave the way it should, get it looked at before school starts.

## Make Sure the Opener Has Battery Backup

This one is easy to overlook but particularly relevant in Michigan, where power outages from ice storms, heavy snow, and winter weather happen regularly.

If your opener loses power and doesn't have a battery backup, the garage door isn't opening — period. An emergency release cord lets you operate the door manually from inside, but a teenager coming home from school doesn't have access to that from outside. They're stuck in the driveway until the power comes back or someone gets home.

Many newer LiftMaster, Chamberlain, and Genie openers have built-in battery backup that kicks in automatically during an outage. If yours doesn't, a battery backup accessory is available for compatible models.

Check your opener's documentation or the manufacturer's website to see whether backup is available for your unit. If your opener is more than 10 or 12 years old and doesn't have this feature, it may be worth factoring into your thinking the next time the opener comes up for replacement.

In the meantime, make sure your teenager knows what to do if the power is out and the door won't open — whether that's calling you, going to a neighbor, or having a backup plan for entry through another door.

---

## Know How to Handle a Door Frozen to the Ground

This one applies specifically to Michigan winters and it catches a lot of homeowners off guard.

When temperatures drop and moisture gets under the bottom seal, the door can freeze to the concrete overnight. A teenager who hits the opener button in the morning and the door doesn't budge may assume something is broken — but the opener is just straining against ice.

Make sure your teenager knows that if the door won't budge in the morning, they should come back inside and let you know rather than standing outside waiting or trying to force it. Running the opener repeatedly against a frozen door strains the motor and can cause real damage.

---

## Set a Good PIN

If you're programming a keypad for the first time or changing an existing code before school starts, choose a PIN that your teenager can remember reliably but isn't obvious to someone watching them enter it.

Avoid birthdays, address numbers, and simple patterns like 1111 or 1234. A random four-digit number that your teenager practices a few times before school starts is the right call. And remind them — the code stays in the family. It's not something to share with friends, no matter how convenient it might seem in the moment.

---

A garage door that works reliably, has fresh batteries in the keypad, and has confirmed working safety features is one less thing to think about during the school year. Most of the items on this list take five minutes or less.
