# How to Get Your Garage Door Ready for a Michigan Winter

Winter is hard on a garage door. Cold stiffens rubber, thickens lubricant, and makes metal contract. Snow and ice pile up where the door meets the ground. And the door is used more than ever, because nobody wants to scrape a windshield or carry groceries through a blizzard when they could pull into a garage.

The good news is that most winter garage door problems are preventable. A little attention now, before the deep cold sets in, can save you from a door that won't open on the coldest morning of the year. Here's a practical checklist for getting your door ready.

---

## Why Cold Weather Causes Problems

A few things happen to a garage door when temperatures drop:

- **Metal contracts.** Springs, tracks, and hardware shrink slightly, which can change how the system fits together and how much tension it carries.
- **Lubricant thickens.** Grease and oil get stiff, which adds friction to every moving part.
- **Rubber hardens.** Weatherstripping and bottom seals lose flexibility and stop sealing well.
- **Moisture freezes.** Water that gets under the door or into the tracks can turn to ice.
- **The opener works harder.** Every added bit of friction makes the motor and drive system labor more.

None of this is a problem for a door in good shape. But a door that's already worn, unbalanced, or neglected will often show its weaknesses the first time it gets truly cold.

---

## 1. Test the Balance

Springs carry nearly all the weight of your door, and the opener is only supposed to guide it. If the spring tension is off, the opener is quietly doing a job it wasn't built for, and cold weather makes that harder.

To check balance, close the door, then pull the red emergency release cord to disconnect the opener. Lift the door by hand to about waist height and let go. A balanced door stays put or moves only a few inches. If it slams down or flies up, the springs need attention.

Springs are also more likely to break in cold weather, since cold metal is less forgiving and a worn spring that has been hanging on all summer can finally let go. If your springs are 7 to 10 years old or the door has seen heavy use, now is a good time to have them looked at.

Adjusting or replacing springs is not a DIY job. They hold a huge amount of stored energy, so leave it to a technician.

---

## 2. Lubricate the Moving Parts

Fresh lubricant helps the whole system move smoothly through the cold. Use products meant for the job: white lithium grease on springs, hinges, and bearing plates, and silicone spray on nylon rollers and rubber. Avoid WD-40 as a long-term lubricant, since it evaporates and can leave a residue that attracts dirt.

## 3. Inspect and Replace the Weatherstripping and Bottom Seal

The seal at the bottom of the door is your first line of defense against snow, slush, wind, and cold. A seal that's cracked, hardened, or torn lets all of that in, and moisture that gets in can freeze the door to the floor.

Look along the bottom of the closed door for daylight, cracks, flat spots, or sections that have pulled out of the retainer. Check the weatherstripping along the sides and top too. If anything looks worn, replace it before the cold arrives. Rubber seals generally hold up better than vinyl in a Michigan winter.

## 4. Check Your Remotes, Keypad, and Wall Control

Cold drains batteries faster. Swap in fresh batteries in your remotes and keypad before winter hits, and keep a spare. If your remote is clipped to the visor and you're noticing reduced range on cold mornings, it's often the battery.

Test the wall control and keypad too, so you're not troubleshooting at 6 a.m. in the dark.

---

## 5. Look at Your Opener

An opener that has been struggling all year will struggle more in the cold. Listen to it run. Grinding, straining, slow starts, or a door that stops partway are signs it's working harder than it should.

If your opener has a battery backup, make sure it's working. Winter storms and ice events cause power outages, and without backup power the door won't open with the opener. Know where your emergency release cord is, and make sure everyone in the house knows how to use it.

---

## 6. Test the Safety Features

Winter is a good time to confirm that your safety features are working. Two quick tests:

- **Auto-reverse:** Lay a 2x4 flat on the floor under the door and close it. The door should reverse as soon as it touches the board.
- **Photo eyes:** Close the door and wave your foot through the beam near the floor. The door should stop and reverse.

Snow, frost, and cobwebs can interfere with the sensors, so wipe the lenses and make sure they're pointed at each other.

---

## 7. Keep the Door From Freezing to the Floor

A door frozen to the concrete is one of the most common winter calls there is. Moisture under the seal freezes overnight, and when the opener tries to lift the door it strains against the ice.

A few ways to reduce the odds:

- Replace a worn seal so less moisture gets in
- Keep the floor under the door clear of puddles and slush
- Shovel and clear snow away from the base of the door
- Sweep out water after you park a wet vehicle if you can

If the door is already frozen, don't keep hitting the button. Running the opener against a frozen door can strain the motor and damage hardware. Break the ice free from the bottom first, then open it.

---

## 8. Mind the Snow and Ice Outside

Snow pushed up against the door can freeze to it. Clear the area at the base of the door after a storm. Be careful with snowblowers and shovels near the bottom panel and seal, because a bumped panel or bent retainer causes gaps.

Go easy on ice melt, too. Salt and de-icer can degrade seals and corrode the bottom of the door and hardware over time. Rinse the area when you can.

---

## 9. Clear Out the Tracks and Hardware

Wipe down the tracks and look at the rollers and hinges. Debris, dirt, and old grease buildup collects along the tracks and can stiffen in the cold. Don't lubricate the tracks themselves. Just keep them clean. Tighten any loose hardware you find, since vibration works bolts loose over time and cold weather can make problems from loose hardware more noticeable.

---

## A Quick Winter Checklist

- Balance test the door
- Lubricate springs, hinges, and rollers with the right products
- Inspect the bottom seal and weatherstripping
- Replace batteries in the remotes and keypad
- Check the opener and battery backup
- Test auto-reverse and the photo eyes
- Clear snow and ice from the base of the door
- Tighten loose hardware and keep the tracks clean

---

## When to Call a Pro

Most of this checklist is easy to do yourself. Call a technician if:

- The door is out of balance or feels heavy
- Springs or cables look worn, frayed, or damaged
- The door is crooked or unevenly closing
- The opener is grinding, straining, or inconsistent
- Safety features don't respond the way they should

A professional tune-up before winter checks all of this at once, and catching a worn spring or fraying cable now beats finding it on a sub-zero morning.
