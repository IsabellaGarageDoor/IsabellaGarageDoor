# Gap Under Your Garage Door? Here's What's Causing It and How to Fix It

You notice it on a bright afternoon or a dark night with the porch light on: a strip of daylight under your closed garage door. Or maybe you notice it other ways. Leaves keep blowing into the garage, water pools by the door after a storm, the garage is freezing in January, or you found evidence that a mouse has been coming and going.

A gap under the garage door is one of the most common problems homeowners run into, and the cause isn't always the one you'd guess. Sometimes it's a worn-out seal. Sometimes it's the concrete. Sometimes the door itself isn't sitting level. The fix depends entirely on which one you're dealing with, so the first step is figuring out what's actually going on.

---

## Why a Small Gap Matters

A little daylight might not seem like a big deal, but a gap under the door lets in more than light.

- **Pests.** Mice can squeeze through openings as small as a quarter inch. A gap under the door is an open invitation.
- **Water and snowmelt.** Rain and melting snow get pushed in under the door, which can damage stored items and leave the floor wet or icy.
- **Cold air.** In a Michigan winter, a gap is a steady draft, which makes an attached garage colder and can make the rooms next to it harder to heat.
- **Debris.** Leaves, dirt, and dust blow in and collect along the door.
- **Security.** A larger gap makes it easier for someone to see in, and in extreme cases to get a tool underneath.

---

## Step 1: Figure Out What Kind of Gap You Have

Before you buy anything, look closely at the gap. Where it shows up and what it looks like tells you a lot.

Close the door, then walk the full width of the bottom edge from inside the garage. If it's hard to see, have someone shine a flashlight along the outside at night, or look for daylight during the day.

**An even gap across the whole width** usually points to a worn bottom seal or an opener setting that's stopping the door short of the floor.

**A gap on one side only** usually means either the floor slopes away on that side or the door isn't sitting level.

**A gap in the middle** (or a gap that appears in the middle and disappears at the ends) usually points to a floor that's higher or lower in the center, or a door panel that's bowing.

**Gaps in a few spots** often mean a damaged seal with missing or crumbled sections, or low spots in the concrete.

Next, here are the most common causes in more detail.

---

## Cause 1: A Worn or Damaged Bottom Seal

This is the most common cause by far, and the easiest to fix.

Bottom seals take a beating. They compress against concrete every time the door closes, they get baked by the sun, they freeze and thaw, and they pick up salt and grit. Over time, rubber and vinyl harden, crack, flatten, and tear, and they stop conforming to the floor.

**How to check:** Look for cracks, flat spots, missing chunks, stiffness, or sections pulling out of the retainer. If the seal is rigid instead of soft and springy, it's done.

**The fix:** Replace the seal. For most doors this is a straightforward project that takes under an hour. We cover the different types of seals and the replacement process step by step in our guide to garage door bottom seals.

---

## Cause 2: Uneven Concrete

Concrete doesn't stay perfectly flat forever. In Michigan, frost heave, settling, and years of freezing and thawing can shift a garage slab so that the floor under the door is no longer level.

**How to check:** Compare the gap along the width of the door. If the gap changes gradually from one side to the other, or opens up in the middle, and the door itself looks straight, the floor may be the cause. Lay a straight edge or level on the floor along the door's path to see how much the slab dips or rises.

**The fixes:**

- **A taller or more flexible seal.** A bulb-style seal or a taller flap seal can bridge a small, consistent gap.
- **A threshold seal.** This is a rubber strip glued to the floor so the door closes against it. It can fill a larger gap and works well on slopes. It needs to be installed carefully, and it can collect snow, ice, and debris.
- **Patching the concrete.** Low spots can sometimes be filled with a concrete patch or self-leveling compound. This is a bigger project, and if the slab has moved a lot, it's worth getting advice before spending money.

If the floor has sunk badly or is heaving, a seal fix is a patch, not a cure.

---

## Cause 3: The Door Isn't Sitting Level

Sometimes the floor and the seal are fine. The door itself is the problem.

A door that sits lower on one side than the other will leave a gap on the high side. This usually comes from the lift system rather than from the panels.

**What can cause it:**

- A cable that has slipped on its drum, or cables that are wound unevenly
- An imbalance between the springs or between the two sides of the system
- A broken cable or a weakened spring
- Loose hinge or strut hardware letting the panels shift (we cover that in our guide to loose hinge and strut bolts)

**How to check:** Place a level on the bottom edge of the closed door. If it reads noticeably off, the door isn't sitting level. Look at both lift cables. If one looks slack or one side hangs lower, there's a problem in the lift system.

**The fix:** Tightening loose hinge and strut bolts is a fair DIY check. But anything involving the cables, the drums, or the springs is a job for a technician. Those parts are under serious tension and are not safe to adjust without the right tools and training. A cable that has jumped off its drum, for example, is a quick fix for someone who knows what to look for, and a risky one for someone who doesn't.

---

## Cause 4: The Opener's Down Limit Is Set Too High

Every opener has a setting that tells it how far to lower the door before stopping. If that limit is set too high, the opener stops the door before it reaches the floor, leaving an even gap across the full width.

**How to check:** The gap is consistent across the whole door, and the seal looks fine. The door closes smoothly and simply stops short.

**The fix:** Adjust the down travel limit on the opener, usually with a screw or button on the motor unit. Check your opener's manual for the exact procedure for your model, and make small adjustments, testing after each one. After any limit or force adjustment, always re-test the auto-reverse by laying a 2x4 flat in the doorway and confirming the door reverses when it touches the board. Never leave a door with a limit or force setting you haven't tested.

---

## Cause 5: A Bent Bottom Panel or Damaged Retainer

If something bumped the bottom of the door, such as a vehicle, a lawn mower, or a snowblower, the bottom panel or the retainer that holds the seal may be bent or dented. A bent retainer can't hold the seal evenly against the floor, and a dented panel can leave a wavy gap.

**How to check:** Look closely along the bottom edge. Dents, waves, or a retainer that's pulled away from the panel are easy to spot.

**The fix:** A bent retainer can usually be replaced along with the seal. A damaged panel is a bigger repair and may need a technician to assess whether it can be fixed or needs to be replaced.

---

## Quick Reference

| What you see | Most likely cause | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Even gap across the whole width | Worn seal or high down limit | Replace seal, or adjust the limit |
| Gap on one side | Sloped floor or door not level | Threshold seal, or have the lift system checked |
| Gap in the middle | Uneven concrete or bowed panel | Taller seal, threshold seal, or panel check |
| Gaps in a few spots | Damaged seal or low spots in the floor | Replace seal, patch concrete |
| Wavy or dented bottom edge | Bent panel or retainer | Replace retainer, or have the panel assessed |

---

## When to Call a Pro

Plenty of gap problems are an easy weekend fix. It's worth getting help if:

- A new seal doesn't solve it, or the gap keeps returning
- One side of the door sits lower than the other
- You see a slack cable, a cable off its drum, or anything wrong with the springs
- The bottom panel is damaged or the retainer is badly bent
- The floor has shifted significantly and you're unsure what to do about it

A gap that shows up suddenly, especially with one side hanging low, can be a sign of a mechanical problem, not just a worn seal. If your door is crooked or uneven, stop using it until it's been looked at.
